Top Story

A voting-rights coalition sues Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin to block armed ICE agents from polling places

Five plaintiffs invoke a Civil War-era criminal election law to challenge the administration's "Polling Place Policy" days before it would take effect this midterm cycle

The League of United Latin American Citizens, Common Cause, Common Cause Education Fund, UnidosUS, and the City and County of Denver, Colorado sued Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, the U.S. Department of Homeland Security, Acting ICE Director David Venturella, and U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement on September 10 in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia.

The complaint centers on what it calls the "Polling Place Policy": Secretary Mullin's September 1 confirmation that armed ICE officers may conduct civil immigration enforcement — including serving warrants and detaining people suspected of being unlawfully present — at polling places during the 2026 midterm election. The plaintiffs argue this violates 18 U.S.C. § 592, a law passed in the final months of the Civil War that makes it a federal crime for officials to place armed men at polling places except to repel an armed invasion. The complaint traces the statute's history to congressional concern over executive-branch overreach into elections, and cites reporting that ICE has unlawfully detained more than 170 U.S. citizens in recent years as the basis for its claim that the policy would chill lawful voters.

Plaintiffs are seeking a declaration that the policy is unlawful and an injunction barring armed federal officers from polling places, ballot dropboxes, and curbside voting locations — during early voting and on Election Day itself — for any reason other than repelling an armed invasion, plus costs and attorneys' fees.

Read the complaint: League of United Latin American Citizens et al. v. Mullin et al., No. 1:26-cv-03157 (D.D.C., filed Sept. 10, 2026)
New Litigation

A hospital's decade-old Medicare dispute reaches HHS Secretary Kennedy, and the Associated Press sues the CIA over a sexual-misconduct cover-up inquiry

Thirty new lawsuits naming a federal agency or official were filed in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia over the past three days — the venue where the great majority of such litigation nationwide gets filed, since it's where Congress directed most challenges to federal agency action. Beyond the lead story, three more suits stand out individually; the rest of the window was the usual routine volume of individual FOIA requests and visa-processing-delay suits, rolled up below.

  • Baylor Scott & White All Saints Medical Center – Fort Worth sued HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. over a July decision by HHS's Provider Reimbursement Review Board dismissing the hospital's challenge to how a Medicare contractor counted Medicaid-eligible patient days — the "Medicaid Fraction" used to calculate Disproportionate Share Hospital payments — for a cost year ending back in 2016. The hospital calls the dismissal a "willful refusal to follow clear, unambiguous federal law" and seeks judicial review under the Administrative Procedure Act. Complaint (No. 1:26-cv-03142).
  • The Associated Press and its reporter James Mustian sued the Central Intelligence Agency under the Freedom of Information Act to force release of the CIA Inspector General's investigation into former CIA officer Brian Jeffrey Raymond, who pleaded guilty in 2021 to sexual abuse and transporting obscene material — specifically records on how the agency handled misconduct complaints against him during his employment and overseas postings. Mustian first requested the records in October 2023; the CIA has not produced them. Complaint (No. 1:26-cv-03131).
  • The United States sued the District of Columbia, the Board of Trustees of the University of the District of Columbia, and D.C. State Superintendent of Education Antoinette Mitchell, arguing D.C.'s in-state tuition and financial-aid rules unlawfully extend benefits to undocumented immigrants in violation of federal law barring states from doing so without an explicit local statute. Complaint (No. 1:26-cv-03166).
  • Also filed against federal agencies, complaint text not available to read for either: the American Federation of State, County and Municipal Employees, AFL-CIO brought an Administrative Procedure Act challenge against the U.S. Department of Labor (No. 1:26-cv-03162), and Better Markets, Inc. filed a federal-question challenge against the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (No. 1:26-cv-03163).

Also filed: 10 new Freedom of Information Act suits against federal agencies, two of them from Judicial Watch, Inc. (against the Departments of Homeland Security and Justice) and one from the American Center for Law and Justice (against the Department of Education) — the rest from individual plaintiffs against the Departments of the Interior, the Army, Homeland Security, and Justice, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement, the National Science Foundation, and the National Institutes of Health. Separately, 11 new suits over delayed visa, green-card, or naturalization adjudications named Secretary of State Marco Rubio and other State Department/U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services officials as defendants — routine volume for this category, no single filing stood out from the rest.

Coverage window: September 8–11, 2026, in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia only — the venue for the great majority of litigation against federal agencies/officials nationwide, but a suit against a regional agency office filed in a different district (e.g. a facility-specific suit filed in a border-state court) won't appear here.
