Top Story

A senior Justice Department official and the District government are named in a thin day for new cabinet-related filings

Six new cases clear this edition's scope today, but PACER's paywall means none of the underlying complaints are readable yet.

New filings in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia over the past three days include a lawsuit naming Todd Blanche, the Deputy Attorney General of the United States, and a separate civil-rights suit against the District of Columbia government itself. Both are brand new — filed within the last day — and neither complaint has been made freely available through CourtListener's RECAP archive yet, so today's coverage is limited to what the docket captions and case-management fields confirm.

The Estate of Randall Garcia filed suit against Blanche on September 16, coded by the court as an Administrative Procedure Act case seeking judicial review of an agency decision (5 U.S.C. § 701). The docket does not yet identify which agency decision is being challenged or what relief is sought, and no complaint text is publicly available to confirm the underlying facts.

Separately, a plaintiff identified in the docket only as Ford sued the District of Columbia government on September 16 under 42 U.S.C. § 1983, the federal civil-rights statute that allows suits against government bodies for constitutional violations. As with the Blanche case, the complaint itself is not yet available, so the specific conduct alleged cannot be confirmed.

A third notable filing, Greenville Rancheria — a federally recognized tribal government in California — sued the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services on September 16 over what the docket codes as an Indian tribal rights dispute (25 U.S.C. § 640), an area where the department's Indian Health Service is the ordinary defendant. Details on the specific dispute are below.

New Litigation

A tribal government sues HHS, and two thin cabinet-adjacent filings round out the day

Beyond the two cases above, one additional distinctive filing and a small handful of routine ones cleared this edition's scope. As with the top story, RECAP has not yet made any of today's complaints freely available, so these summaries rely on docket captions and case-management codes only.

  • Greenville Rancheria, a federally recognized Indian tribe based in Northern California, sued the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services on September 16 (docket 1:26-cv-03241), a case the court has coded under the Indian Tribal Rights statute (25 U.S.C. § 640), which governs federal funding and contracting for tribal health programs. Complaint text is not available, docket caption only — the specific dispute (for example, a funding-agreement or contract-support-cost disagreement) cannot be confirmed.

Two additional, apparently routine Federal Tort Claims Act suits against the United States were also filed and clear this edition's scope: Munchel v. United States of America (1:26-cv-03229, filed Sept. 15) and Taylor v. United States (1:26-cv-03213, filed Sept. 14). Complaint text is not available for either; the nature of the underlying personal-injury or property claims cannot be confirmed from the docket alone.

One new mandamus suit over delayed visa adjudication was filed today: Adla v. Rubio (1:26-cv-03250), naming Secretary of State Marco Rubio in his official capacity, part of the steady, largely routine stream of suits compelling State Department/USCIS action on stalled visa petitions.
