Top Story

Southern Poverty Law Center sues DOJ inspector general over stalled records on a federal prison use-of-force incident

The suit says the Justice Department watchdog has sat on a Freedom of Information Act request for more than five months.

The Southern Poverty Law Center, Inc. sued the Office of the Inspector General of the U.S. Department of Justice in the U.S. District Court for the District of Columbia on September 23, 2026, alleging the watchdog office has failed to respond to a Freedom of Information Act request for records about a use-of-force incident inside a federal prison. The case, filed under 5 U.S.C. § 552, is Southern Poverty Law Center v. Office of the Inspector General, No. 1:26-cv-03325.

According to the FOIA request itself, filed as an exhibit to the complaint, the organization's attorney, Krista Dolan, wrote to the Office of the Inspector General on April 8, 2026, seeking all records relating to a use-of-force incident on or about August 28, 2025, at FCI Coleman Low, a federal Bureau of Prisons facility in Wildwood, Florida, involving an inmate identified as Ervin Allen, Register No. 49595-039. The request sought investigative reports, witness statements, video or photographic evidence, and communications between the inspector general's office and Bureau of Prisons staff about the incident, along with a public-interest fee waiver.

A second exhibit shows the inspector general's Office of General Counsel acknowledged the request on April 10, 2026, assigning it tracking number 26-OIG-224 and warning that a "significant FOIA backlog" could delay processing. The complaint's own text — including its specific claims and requested relief — was not available through the RECAP archive; only the civil cover sheet and the two exhibits (the request letter and the acknowledgment) could be read directly. Suits under this cause of action typically ask the court to order the agency to complete an adequate search and produce responsive records, as authorized by 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(B).

The case is notable both for its subject — federal oversight of force used against a person in Bureau of Prisons custody — and for illustrating a recurring complaint in FOIA litigation against the Justice Department: agencies citing backlogs to justify delays well beyond the statute's 20-business-day response deadline.
