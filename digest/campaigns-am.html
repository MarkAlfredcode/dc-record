Top Story
Senator Jim Justice Discloses a $50 Million-Plus Stock Sale Hours After the Senate Shelves a Vote on Banning Congressional Stock Trading
The two events happened on the same Senate day, in unrelated proceedings — but the timing captures exactly what a trading ban for members of Congress is meant to address.

Senator Jim Justice (Republican, West Virginia) disclosed a partial sale of non-public stock in Greenbrier Hotel Corporation and Affiliates — his family's flagship hospitality business in White Sulphur Springs, West Virginia — valued at “Over $50,000,000,” the top disclosure bracket on the Senate's Periodic Transaction Report form, meaning the true sale price could be substantially higher. The transaction is dated August 14, 2026, and Justice reported it as held through a family trust identified only as the “2012 Trust.” He filed the disclosure on September 28, 2026 — the 45th day after the transaction, the outer edge of the STOCK Act's disclosure window, so the filing is timely but was made at the deadline rather than promptly.

The disclosure landed on the same Senate day that Majority Leader John Thune moved to proceed to H.R. 7008, the Stop Insider Trading Act, which would impose new stock-trading restrictions on members of Congress and their spouses and dependents. Thune filed a cloture motion — signed by seventeen Republican senators including Ted Cruz, Rick Scott of Florida, and Cynthia Lummis — that would have set up a Wednesday vote to limit debate on the motion to proceed. Hours later, according to the Congressional Record, Thune withdrew the motion to proceed outright, before any vote occurred.

The two events are not connected by anything in the record beyond timing. Justice's transaction involves privately held stock in his own family's company, not a publicly traded security, and the disclosure obligation he satisfied already exists under current law; H.R. 7008 would go further by barring members from trading covered securities at all, a restriction that would not obviously reach a closely held family business the same way. Still, the juxtaposition is a reminder of what's animating the underlying debate — a sitting senator disclosing an eight-figure-plus transaction in the same 24 hours the chamber quietly stepped back, without a recorded vote, from tightening its own members' trading rules.

Sitting Lawmakers
Two Incumbent House Members Answer Compliance Letters Over Contribution Designations

Two sitting representatives' campaign committees filed Form 99 responses to FEC Requests for Additional Information in this window, both defending how contributions were designated between primary and general elections rather than disclosing anything improper.

  • Rep. Mike Kennedy (Republican, Utah's 4th District) — Mike Kennedy for Utah filed a lengthy legal response to an RFAI questioning contributions designated for the 2026 Republican primary, even though Kennedy secured the nomination outright at Utah's convention and no primary was ultimately held. The committee agreed to refund or redesignate primary-designated money received after the April 25 convention, but argued it can keep money received before the convention, citing two prior FEC precedents (Friends of Mike Lee, 2017, and Friends of Mia Love, 2018) in which the Commission let committees retain pre-convention primary contributions because, at the time they were raised, another candidate could still have qualified for the primary ballot through Utah's separate signature-gathering process — which is exactly what happened here, the committee says, until that process concluded the day before the convention. Filing.
  • Rep. Scott Fitzgerald (Republican, Wisconsin's 5th District) — filed 09/27, not indexed by FEC's filings API until after yesterday's edition had already closed. Scott Fitzgerald for Congress responded to two RFAIs covering its 12-Day Pre-Primary and July Quarterly reports: a batch of contributions mistakenly flagged as conduit/earmarked have been corrected to show only direct receipt; two PAC contributions wrongly split between the primary and general elections have been fixed and the amended reports filed; most excessive contributions the committee made to other candidates have already been refunded, with one still outstanding while the committee awaits a response from that committee's treasurer; and one contribution that looked like it came from an unregistered committee was confirmed to be a permissible state-level committee contribution. Filing.

Senate Passes the College-Sports NIL Bill 77-22 and Opens, Then Drops, a Cloture Push on a Congressional Stock-Trading Ban; House Holds a Two-Minute Pro Forma Session but Introduces 30 Bills and Resolutions

The Senate had a long floor day September 28: it passed S. 4668, the Protect College Sports Act, 77-22, after voting on five amendments (three adopted, two rejected) covering foreign-financing disclosure, conference-merger limits, ticket-price caps, and post-eligibility insurance funding. It also passed a stack of other bills, including discharging and passing the Common Cents Act (H.R. 10167, to stop minting the penny and require cash transactions to round to the nearest five cents) and the Terrorism Risk Insurance Program Reauthorization Act (S. 4395). Separately, Majority Leader John Thune moved to proceed to H.R. 7008, the Stop Insider Trading Act — see this edition's Top Story — filed cloture, then withdrew the motion to proceed before any vote occurred. The Senate also agreed to S. Res. 913, honoring the late former Senator Nancy Kassebaum of Kansas, and adjourned early as a further mark of respect for her.
